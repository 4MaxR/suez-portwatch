    About the data

    Where the numbers come from and how they were processed

    IMF PortWatch is a platform from the International Monetary Fund and the University of Oxford that turns ship-position signals (AIS) into daily counts of ships passing 28 maritime chokepoints and calling at about 2,000 ports, with an estimate of the cargo each ship carried. It is published as open ArcGIS feature layers, which this project downloads without a key.

    Glossary

    AIS
    Automatic Identification System: the position, speed and identity signal that cargo ships broadcast. Received by coastal stations and satellites.
    Transit
    A ship crossing a chokepoint's boundary. A ship is not counted again at the same chokepoint within 48 hours.
    Port call
    A ship entering a port to load or unload. Stops under 5 hours with no change in draft (bunkering, anchoring) are excluded.
    Cargo tonnes
    PortWatch's estimate of cargo on board: how deep the ship sits in the water (its payload share) times its deadweight tonnage.
    DWT
    Deadweight tonnage: the most a ship can carry, in metric tonnes.
    Baseline
    January–October 2023: the ten months before the first Houthi attack on merchant shipping (19 Nov 2023).
    SCA
    Suez Canal Authority, the canal's operator. Its published revenue anchors the proxy.

    Limitations

    • Estimates, not manifests. Ship counts come from AIS tracks; cargo tonnes are modelled from draft and ship size. Neither is a customs or canal-authority record.
    • Five cargo types only. Container, dry bulk, general cargo, ro-ro and tanker. Passenger ships, naval vessels and small craft are not counted, and LNG carriers sit inside "tanker".
    • Revenue is a proxy. It assumes revenue moves with tonnage. Toll rates, rebates and the mix of ship sizes all changed after 2023, so it is a scale estimate, not an accounting figure.
    • Timing, not proof of cause. The drop lines up with the attacks, but the data cannot separate the security effect from freight rates, insurance costs or carrier network decisions.
    • Noisy single days. AIS reception gaps create one-day spikes and dips, especially at the Cape. All findings use monthly or multi-month averages.
    • Port boundaries are PortWatch's. Port Said may combine the east and west terminals; El Sokhna's gains may partly reflect new terminal capacity, which this data cannot see.
